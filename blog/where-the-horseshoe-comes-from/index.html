Why the map of visible color is a horseshoe

November 13, 2026 · 21 min read

I recently got into virtual photography, which is taking photos inside video games. Editing my shots is what keeps sending me down these color rabbit holes.

This is part 2 of a series about color. In What does RGB 255, 0, 0 actually mean? I could say what the triangles on the map of visible color mean, but the map itself, the horseshoe, was “magic for now”. So I did what I do these days: I sat down with Claude and asked until it stopped being magic. Nothing I work on needs this. I just got curious, and I’m sure there are other people like me who would enjoy it too.

This article is that chat, tidied up, with widgets Claude built so you can play with the answers. Here is what I asked:

  • Why does the eye need exactly three numbers?
  • What exactly do x and y mean on the color map?
  • Why isn’t the horseshoe centered at 0,0?
  • Where does the horseshoe come from? Why is that the limit, not bigger or smaller?
  • Would five lights fix it?
  • Can a library do the conversion for me?

Disclaimer: Claude wrote this from our chat, and I’m just the guy who asked the questions. Some of it may be wrong. I’ve marked what Claude explained and what we checked in code. If you catch a mistake, tell me in the comments.

Key takeaways:

  • Your eye turns any light into three numbers, one per cone type, so three numbers are enough for a picture.
  • x and y are shares, so brightness drops out. White sits in the middle of the map, not at 0,0.
  • The horseshoe is every color a person can see. Nothing real sits outside it.
  • The shape depends on the chart you draw it on.
  • A fifth light adds reach, not more numbers, and not much reach either.

Why does the eye need three numbers?

Short answer: your eye has three kinds of cone cells, called L, M and S (long, medium and short wavelengths). Each kind reports one number, how much light it caught. So your eye turns any light into three numbers, and three numbers is all a picture needs to carry. That’s why every image format has three channels.

Here are the three kinds, each scaled so its peak is 1 (the data is from CVRL). Each curve says how strongly that cone type reacts to a single wavelength. The strip under the chart shows what each wavelength looks like, as close as your screen can get:

Interactive illustration (cone-sensitivities). Open this article on antondanshin.com to try it.

Look at how much the L and M curves overlap. Around yellow-green both are close to their peak, so no light can wake up only one of them. Light always wakes up its neighbors.

Could a light wake up only one cone type, a “pure L”, say? No real light can. It would be a color more saturated than anything in the horseshoe. So even a screen with perfect single-wavelength primaries can’t put a primary there. The curved edge of the horseshoe is that overlap made visible.

At 550 nm the L cone responds at 94% of its peak and the M cone at 98%. Both are above 90% from 545 to 560 nm. The curves are Stockman and Sharpe’s 2° cone fundamentals, from the CVRL link above.

What do x and y mean?

Part 1 said the map doesn’t use red, green and blue. Instead it has two axes, x across and y up. Here it is, the same chart as in part 1. Ignore the three triangles for now (they are screens, from part 1), and look at where the colors sit: red at the right end of the horseshoe, green at the top, blue at the bottom left. Also look at where the corner at 0,0 is, and where the white dot is.

Interactive illustration (gamut-chart). Open this article on antondanshin.com to try it.

This is where I got super confused. Behind that chart there are X, Y and Z, and also x, y and z. Same letters, big and small, and they are not the same thing.

  • Capital X, Y, Z are three numbers for a light, a remix of what the cones report:
    • Y is brightness. The CIE built it to match how bright the eye finds each wavelength.
    • Z is close to the S cone’s response, so it is mostly “how much blue-ish light”.
    • X is the leftover mix. It is roughly red-ish, but it is not “red”. It was chosen so the numbers come out right, not because it means something.
  • Lowercase x, y, z are shares: x = X / (X + Y + Z), y = Y / (X + Y + Z) and z = Z / (X + Y + Z). The map’s axes are x and y.

Claude explained this part to me. We haven’t checked it in code.

Why shares? A light has three numbers, and the map is flat. So the map shows how the light is split between X, Y and Z, not how much of it there is. The three shares add up to 1, so two are enough, and the third, z, is whatever is left. Turn the light up and all three numbers grow equally, so the shares stay the same. Brightness drops out.

That answered something that bugged me: with three cones, how can two numbers be enough? For color they are. Brightness is the third number, and the map just leaves it out. A dim orange and a bright orange land on the same spot.

Why isn’t the horseshoe centered at 0,0?

Because 0,0 isn’t the middle of anything. It’s a corner. It doesn’t mean “no light”. It means all of the light is in the third share, Z, and no real light is like that. The shares add up to 1, so x and y together can never be more than 1. That cuts the map into a triangle, and 0,0 is one corner of it.

So where is the middle? White. A light with the same amount at every wavelength has equal shares, about a third each, so it lands at about a third across and a third up. The horseshoe wraps around that point, and the farther you go from white in any direction, the more saturated the color gets. The dead space outside the horseshoe is the same story as 0,0: shares that would need negative light.

That “white in the middle” isn’t one fixed spot, though. Part 1 had the white point: a screen has to pick which white it means by (255, 255, 255). The equal-light white is a handy middle, but screens don’t use it. Most color spaces pick D65, the midday daylight white, which sits a little off to the side of it. So the map has no single white. Every color space marks its own, and the triangle’s corners are measured around it.

For nerds: where the middle is

Claude and I checked these in code, on the table the horseshoe widget uses (every 5 nm).

Adding up the table with the same weight on every wavelength gives x 0.333 and y 0.333. The edge of the horseshoe runs from x 0.004 to 0.735 and from y 0.005 to 0.834. D65 is at x 0.3127, y 0.3290, the value the widgets use for sRGB, P3 and BT.2020, so it is a little left of the equal-light white and a little below it.

Where does the horseshoe come from?

Part 1 left this as magic. It isn’t. The horseshoe isn’t a limit somebody chose, it comes from measuring, and the argument is short:

  1. Any real light is a mix of wavelengths: some amount of light at each one, never less than nothing.
  2. Cones add things up. A cone’s response to a mix is the sum of its responses to the wavelengths in it. So the three numbers for any light are the single-wavelength numbers added up in those amounts.
  3. Take one pure wavelength at a time, from violet to deep red, find its three numbers and plot where it lands on the map. It draws a curve, the curved edge of the horseshoe. (The name for it is the spectral locus.)
  4. Mixing lights lands you somewhere between them, so every mix of lights on that curve lands inside the shape the curve encloses. All real colors fill it.

Slide through the wavelengths and watch it get drawn. The bars show how strongly each cone type responds:

Interactive illustration (draw-the-horseshoe). Open this article on antondanshin.com to try it.

So the horseshoe is what you can mix from a paint box with one tube for every wavelength. That also answers why it’s the limit:

  • Why not bigger? Outside would need a light purer than a single wavelength, meaning negative light at some wavelengths.
  • Why not smaller? Every point inside can be made by some mix of real light.

Why that particular shape

Walk along the edge and each stretch has a reason. From red to yellow it’s nearly a straight line: the S cones hardly respond there, so only L and M change, and mixing two things gives a straight line. Around cyan-green the edge bends sharply, because the S cones start to matter. That’s the part every display triangle misses the most. From blue down to violet the S cones take over. And the flat bottom edge is the line of purples. No single wavelength makes magenta or purple. They only exist as mixes of the two ends of the spectrum, and mixing two points gives a straight line between them.

For nerds: the numbers behind the edge

Claude and I checked these in code, on the table the widget uses (every 5 nm).

From 570 to 700 nm the edge stays within 0.0001 of a straight line. At 560 nm the S cones respond at under 0.1%; at 500 nm they are at 12% and at 520 nm at 3%. The leftmost point of the curve is at 505 nm (x 0.0039) and its top is at 520 nm (x 0.0743, y 0.8338). The S cones become the strongest of the three below about 485 nm.

One more thing Claude explained to me, which we haven’t checked: the cone curves in the widgets and the table that draws the horseshoe are two different datasets. The table is the 1931 one, and cones weren’t measured yet back then. The two are close but not exact remixes of each other, which is why the story above works as a story.

For nerds: where the data came from

This part is Claude’s explanation, checked against the Wikipedia article on CIE 1931.

In the late 1920s, Wright and Guild ran color-matching experiments with about 17 observers in total (Wright 10, Guild 7). Observers matched a pure test wavelength by adjusting three primary lights. For many wavelengths no match was possible, so a primary had to be added to the test side instead. That is the negative coefficient from the twist in What does RGB 255, 0, 0 actually mean?, observed in a lab. The CIE standardized the averaged result in 1931 as the “standard observer”, so it is an average and individuals deviate from it a little.

The shape depends on the chart

Everything on the map so far uses the 1931 xy chart. Dividing by the total, the shares trick from earlier, removes brightness, and it keeps straight lines straight, which is why a screen’s gamut is a triangle on it. But the chart isn’t even: the same distance on it can be a big difference in one place and invisible in another. The huge green area is partly an artifact of the math, not a sign that we see lots of greens.

There is a later chart that tries to fix this, called u′v′. It’s designed so equal distances look more equal, though it’s still not perfect (see the CIE’s technical note).

I don’t know why I even got there, but since Claude mentioned it, I asked it to illustrate this for me. Slide between the two and watch the same colors change shape:

In simpler terms, same colors, different shape of the horseshoe. Although sRGB covers about the same share on both charts, the wide spaces (like the huge green area I mentioned above) look a bit smaller on the u′v′ chart.

So, the main takeaway here: when you read “covers X% of visible colors” on a spec sheet, it matters which chart they used.

For nerds: the formulas

Claude explained these to me, and we checked the shares in code (the widget shows them).

The shares from earlier are x = X / (X + Y + Z) and y = Y / (X + Y + Z). The later chart is the CIE 1976 one, which the CIE recommends for light sources: u′ = 4x / (−2x + 12y + 3) and v′ = 9y / (−2x + 12y + 3).

Would five lights fix it?

My first guess was that five lights would be a big upgrade. It turned out to be about half right, and the half that surprised me wasn’t the area.

Remember the paint box with a tube for every wavelength? A box with five tubes mixes a pentagon on the map instead of a triangle, and the more tubes you add along the curved edge, the closer you get to the whole horseshoe. Here is what that looks like. We put the first three single-wavelength lights near where BT.2020’s sit, and each new one goes wherever it adds the most area:

Interactive illustration (primaries-count). Open this article on antondanshin.com to try it.

Three well-placed lights already cover about two thirds of the horseshoe, the same as BT.2020 (sRGB is about a third). A fourth one, a cyan-green, adds a lot. The fifth adds only a little, and after that the gains get small. These are areas on the xy chart, which stretches the greens a lot, so don’t read them as “how much we notice”, like I said earlier.

This has been tried:

  • Sharp sold TVs with an extra yellow subpixel (Quattron). Whether it gave the TV a real fourth primary was disputed: the Wikipedia article cites researchers who argued the backlight itself has no fourth primary to drive it.
  • Cinema projectors with six laser primaries exist (Christie’s, for example, an archived copy). They use two sets of red, green and blue, mostly to get 3D working with glasses, not to widen the gamut.
  • In printing, “extended gamut” adds orange, green and violet inks to CMYK (FESPA explains).

Five lights, still three numbers

The bigger surprise was this. I assumed five lights would mean five numbers. They don’t.

Light adds up, so a screen mixes its lights in amounts: some of each, never less than none. The color you see is just the three cone numbers you get from that mix. With three lights there are three amounts to find and three equations, so there’s exactly one answer, and it only works if no amount comes out negative. That’s the triangle wall from part 1. With five lights there are five amounts and still three equations, so lots of different mixes look the same, and the screen has to pick one (the most power-efficient, say). The reachable area is a bigger shape.

A fifth primary adds reach, not more numbers. The signal stays three numbers, and a five-light screen works out its five drive values inside.

For nerds: the same thing as a formula

Claude explained this to me.

If a screen has primaries with cone responses LMS(p₁), LMS(p₂) and so on, then any color it makes is LMS = Σ aᵢ · LMS(primaryᵢ), where aᵢ is how much of each light you turn on, and every aᵢ has to be zero or more. The LMS on the left is three numbers, so that’s three equations.

I got stuck on this one. If the signal stays three numbers, what if the signal had five too? Would the screen do anything with them?

So I asked Claude. Turns out, for what you see, not really. Your eye only reports three numbers, one per cone type. Lots of different five-light mixes end up as the same three numbers in your head, so you can’t tell them apart. The two extra numbers would carry stuff your eye throws away.

And the triangle wall isn’t about having three numbers. It’s about no light being allowed to go below zero. A signal that says “this exact spot on the map of visible color” still needs only three numbers. A five-light screen then works out its own five values from them.

Where more lights could matter: people’s eyes differ a bit, so two people can disagree about whether two colors match. That gets worse with very pure primaries like lasers, and researchers have built displays with more than three primaries to make the disagreement smaller (RIT writes about it for cinema). For games and photos on a screen nobody bothers, at least I think so.

Claude explained this to me. We haven’t checked it in code.

Why it never took over

I asked Claude why every screen is still three-primary. Claude explained these, and I haven’t checked them against sources:

  1. Diminishing returns. Modern lasers and quantum dots already put three primaries close to the edge. BT.2020’s primaries are single wavelengths, and that triangle already covers about two thirds of the horseshoe. What is left is mostly the cyan-greens.
  2. More subpixels means smaller subpixels, so less light gets through.
  3. Content is made for three primaries. sRGB, P3 and BT.2020 are all three-primary targets, so extra primaries need extra work in the display to convert.

Pro Tip: For my own work none of this changes anything. The ceiling that matters is the gamut of my screen (P3) and of where I post (Instagram is effectively sRGB for most viewers), not the three-primary limit.

Can a library do the conversion for me?

The map is only half the job. The other half is getting from a point on it to the numbers in your editor. That takes two steps: a matrix mixes X, Y and Z into amounts of red, green and blue, and then a curve turns those amounts into the familiar 0 to 255. If any amount comes out below zero or above one, the color is outside that screen’s triangle. That’s the triangle wall from part 1, in numbers.

For nerds: from the map to RGB numbers

Claude explained the steps to me, and we use the same formula in our widgets.

A point on the map is only x and y, so first you need a brightness Y to go with it. Then undo the shares:

X = x / y · Y
Z = (1 − x − y) / y · Y

Next comes a 3×3 matrix that turns XYZ into linear RGB, meaning RGB where the numbers are amounts of light. The matrix is built from the color space’s primaries and white point, so every space has its own. For sRGB it is:

R =  3.2410 X − 1.5374 Y − 0.4986 Z
G = −0.9692 X + 1.8760 Y + 0.0416 Z
B =  0.0556 X − 0.2040 Y + 1.0570 Z

The white point goes in as D65, so D65 itself comes out as (1, 1, 1), which is what “this space’s white” means. Last, the transfer curve turns each linear value L into the number you see:

v = 12.92 · L                    when L ≤ 0.0031308
v = 1.055 · L^(1/2.4) − 0.055    otherwise

That 0 to 1 value times 255 is the familiar 0 to 255.

If any of R, G or B comes out below 0 or above 1, the color is outside that space’s triangle. For example, a 520 nm light at Y = 1 (x 0.074, y 0.834) gives R −1.30, G 1.79 and B −0.08 in sRGB. Red would have to be negative light.

You don’t have to write this yourself. I’m a developer, so I asked Claude whether there are JavaScript libraries for converting colors and color spaces. There are, and these are the ones it named. I haven’t tried them. I only checked that the links work and skimmed their pages. The widgets in this article use our own small code, not a library.

  • Color.js covers many color spaces, including P3, Rec. 2020 and OKLab, and can squeeze out-of-gamut colors back into a space. It looks like the most complete one.
  • culori is another full library, with wide-gamut spaces like P3.
  • chroma-js is popular and handy for color scales and palettes. It supports OKLab.
  • colord is tiny and has plugins for more color spaces.
  • color-convert converts between color models, and d3-color comes from the D3 charting world.

Here is the green light from the box above, done with Color.js instead of by hand:

import Color from "colorjs.io"

// a 520 nm light from the map: x 0.074, y 0.834, with brightness Y = 1
const x = 0.074
const y = 0.834
const Y = 1
const light = new Color("xyz-d65", [(x / y) * Y, Y, ((1 - x - y) / y) * Y])

light.to("srgb-linear").coords // [-1.30, 1.79, -0.08], amounts of light
light.to("srgb").inGamut() // false: the red is negative
light.to("srgb").toGamut().toString({ format: "hex" }) // "#87ffc5", the closest sRGB can get

Claude ran this in code (Color.js 0.7.1). The linear numbers match the ones in the box. In Color.js, srgb means the encoded numbers you see, and srgb-linear means the amounts of light, so the numbers to compare are on the second line. The last line gives a color sRGB can actually show, but it is a different color, not the same light.

What I took away

I don’t work with color on this deep a level, and none of this changes how I edit my shots. I went down this hole out of pure curiosity. What surprised me most was the five lights. I expected a big upgrade, and what I found was that five lights still mean three numbers, and the gain over four is small. The next time a spec sheet says a screen “covers 90% of visible colors”, I’ll at least know what that’s measuring, and what it isn’t. I’d rather know than guess.

If you want to poke at it yourself, there is a page with the widgets from this article, with two of them swapped for bigger tools: a gamut explorer (hover anywhere on the map and it tells you which color spaces can show that point) and a playground where you drag your own primaries around and watch how much of visible color they cover.

The horseshoe isn’t magic anymore, but the map also made me notice another bit of magic. Every editor has sliders called hue, saturation and lightness, and I realized I couldn’t say what they measure. They don’t sit on this map at all: they are another way of labeling the same three RGB numbers. That is HSL and HSV: RGB with different knobs, and after it comes the color system that was built to sit on this map and match the eye.
